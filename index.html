ProblemThe ask, and the job it competed with

Users came to manage insurance — not to be sold to.

The Product team wanted Portfolio to do more. Five capabilities needed to be discovered:

Add Policy · CoverRisk · Buy Policy · Family Management · KYP

But Portfolio's primary job was still simple: help users see and manage the insurance they already have. Promotional banners competed with that job.

People land here to check a premium date, find a policy number, see what has lapsed. A banner doesn't fail because it is badly placed. It fails because it interrupts someone mid-task with an offer they have no way to evaluate — and some of them stop opening the section at all.

IF a capability needs adoption → THEN put a banner on the busiest screen  — rejected

Not “how do we promote these features?” but “when does each feature become useful to this user?”

The second question has an answer you can build. It converts promotional slots into conditions — and a condition can be evaluated against a person's actual portfolio.

Business needs adoption.
Users need relevance.

StrategyScreen 02 — the system

We stopped pushing features and started using context.

Information was already being gathered across the product. It simply wasn't being read by the screen that needed it most.

Onboarding → Homepage → Portfolio → Policy Details
  • Understand

    What do we know about this user? Policies held, family members added, CoverRisk report status, each policy's KYP rating, onboarding answers.

  • Prioritise

    What matters most for their situation? Not every gap is equally urgent, and not every product is equally foundational.

  • Guide

    Show one or two useful next actions. Never ten. The ceiling is the design.

  • Adapt

    User acts → context changes → Portfolio changes. This is what separates a system from a smarter banner.

Proof 02Personalisation — the decision layer

We had the user's risk data. We weren't using it at the buying moment.

CoverRisk already collects a detailed picture of the user:

Age · Location · Family · Dependants · Health · Lifestyle · Income · Investments · Property · Expenses

It generates a detailed risk report and recommends an appropriate coverage amount. Previously that intelligence largely stayed inside CoverRisk. The report was the end of the road, while the buying screen — the place where a number would change someone's mind — still showed a category name.

I connected it back to Portfolio. That was the whole intervention: no new data, no new model, just the number placed where the decision happens.

Understand → Recommend → Quantify → Buy

Most people don't know what cover they need. Left alone, they optimise the one number they understand — the premium — and buy the cheapest policy available. Then treatment takes away their income and they discover the cover was never built for their life. That is what critical illness cover exists for, and it is invisible to anyone shopping on price.

So the recommendation names a coverage level and an average premium for it, at their age and health profile. Not a specific insurer. Not a cheap policy.

Don't optimise for the cheapest premium. Help users start with enough protection.

PrioritisationThe recommendation order

We prioritised protection — not the product catalogue.

A recommendation system is only as good as its ranking. I worked with Product and Team leadership to define which insurance needs should come first, and which only make sense once those exist.

CoverRisk does not decide this order. It supplies the coverage amount and the premium estimate. The sequence is a separate decision, and it holds regardless of what the report returns.

Proof 03Screen 06 — behavioural UX

Share Policy needed a reason — not another button.

The question wasn't where should Share Policy go? It was when does sharing become useful enough to act on?

Sharing an insurance policy isn't a convenience. It is a responsibility, and the moment it pays off is the worst moment of someone's life — a nominee hunting for a policy number while grieving. You can't design for that moment. You design ahead of it.

Important policy → Nominee context → Reason → Share

So on Life and Group policies the entry point stopped being a button and became a sentence:

Your nominee may not be able to access this policy easily during an emergency. Share Policy · entry copy

Reason leads. Action follows. And the same principle runs across the whole screen: don't just show an action — explain why it matters now.

Proof 04Screen 07 — family management

Family Overview turns “Add Family” into part of Portfolio management.

One person usually manages insurance for the whole family. But if family policies are scattered, or a member's policy is missing, then premiums, renewals and emergencies all get harder.

So the new Family Overview makes the family visible upfront. It answers three questions at a glance:

  • Who

    Who is covered? Every member, their policy count, their yearly premium.

  • Gap

    Who is missing? An incomplete overview reads as incomplete.

  • Next

    Whose policies still need to be added? The prompt is the section itself.

Family overview → Add family member → Add their policy

The section becomes a natural prompt — instead of another promotional banner asking users to do it. That is the whole thesis applied to an adoption problem: let the structure carry the request.

Proof 05Screen 08 — KYP integration

We brought KYP closer to the policy itself.

Know Your Policy already helped users understand whether their policy was good or carried important limitations. But the feature lived separately from the policies users were actually looking at.

We connected the KYP rating directly to the policy card. Users now see the rating where they already manage the policy, then explore the reasoning behind it:

Good coverage · ICU limits · Room-rent limits · Refill benefits · Other conditions

The rating is a plain verdict, not a number, and it arrives with a count of how many conditions work in the user's favour and how many don't. A room-rent cap is the kind of clause nobody reads and everybody discovers at the worst possible time — the count is what makes it findable at all.

This turns KYP from a separate feature into part of policy understanding.

Screen 08  ·  Policy card + KYP rating

Every policy carries its rating — a plain-language verdict plus a count of what's good and what isn't. Nothing is hidden; the point is that the user finds out now rather than at claim time.

Proof 06Restraint — the entry point I declined

Advisor Portfolio appears when the user has a reason to ask for help.

We deliberately didn't add an Advisor banner to Portfolio. The Homepage already provides that entry point, so users have a direct route. Repeating it here would have added noise without adding access.

Instead, after CoverRisk generates the user's report, we offer an advisor — at the moment when they have context, and probably questions, that justify expert help.

This is a different job from general support. The PRO tier's relationship manager helps with claims, legal questions and whatever else goes wrong; Advisor Portfolio is specifically for guidance on cover. Two distinct needs, so stacking a second advisor entry point here would have blurred both.

Understand your risk → See the report → Get expert guidance

This makes the advisor action feel useful rather than promotional. Declining to place a feature is harder to argue for than placing one — and it is the decision I would defend first.

What I didn't control

The PRO subscription banner on this screen carries over from the previous design and sits outside this scope. It is already being repositioned in an upcoming update. Worth naming: the conditions described here govern the entry points I owned, not every pixel on the screen.

The loopWhy it keeps getting better

Every action creates more context.

Portfolio isn't a collection of independent CTAs. It works as a continuous loop, where each action returns information to the thing that made the recommendation.

User context → Relevant action → New information → Better recommendation → Next useful action
  • Add a policy

    We understand more, and the gap being measured shrinks.

  • Finish CoverRisk

    Recommendations become specific — categories turn into amounts.

  • Add family

    The family picture becomes clearer, and the prompt stops appearing.

  • Explore KYP

    Policy understanding improves, which changes what is worth recommending next.

The Portfolio changes as the user changes.

I worked closely with Product and Design leadership to shape the final strategy, balancing four things that don't automatically agree:

User needs × Product priorities × Business goals × Insurance context

That last one matters more than it sounds. Our product and team leadership carry years of knowledge about this product, its users and the insurance industry — which is why the recommendation order, the protection tiering and the family structure were all worked through with them rather than designed in isolation.

The work wasn't “where should we place the CTA?” It was who should see it, when, why, and what should happen next.
