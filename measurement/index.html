CoverSure/Policy Portfolio/Measurement plan

Measure the claims, not the clicks.

The Portfolio redesign made a set of falsifiable promises. This is how to test each one, what would prove it wrong, and what to change when it is.

The governing idea

Every design decision in this release was a hypothesis. Treat it like one.

"Conditions beat slots." "Reason before action." "The pitch sits below the policies." Each of those is a claim that can be wrong, and each has a number attached that would show it. A dashboard of engagement counts will tell you none of them.

The risk is not measuring too little. It is measuring the wrong thing well — and then optimising toward the banner strategy this release existed to replace.

01 — The one metric that tests the thesis

State transition rate.

The design's own definition of progress is that a condition stops being true. A user with no policies adds one. A family overview completes. An inactive policy gets resolved. A protection gap closes.

So the headline number is: % of users who move to a better portfolio state within 30 days, measured per starting state. Nothing else captures whether the system actually moves people forward rather than merely being looked at.

Why this beats a completion score

"Portfolio completeness" sounds like the right north star, but you cannot observe how many policies a person really owns — only how many they have added. Completeness is therefore unmeasurable and easy to fake by suppressing prompts. Transitions are observable, honest, and directly attributable to the design.

Supporting the headline

  • Prompt resolution rate — of conditions that became true, what share got resolved, by condition type
  • Time to resolution — median days from a condition becoming true to it being resolved
  • Stale condition count — conditions true for more than 60 days. A rising number means the screen is being ignored, and it rises quietly
  • Cover adequacy ratio — see §04. This is the one the business should care most about
02 — The diagnostic ladder

Five questions, in this order.

Most teams jump straight to "did they click". That skips the two questions that actually determine whether a rules-based system works.

The methodological rule that matters most

This design shows different things to different people. Every metric must be cut by portfolio state. An aggregate action rate across users who saw a CoverRisk prompt, an add-family prompt and a lapsed-policy alert is a number about nothing. Averages will hide both your best result and your worst.

04 — The business metric

Cover adequacy ratio.

Of everything here, this is the number I would put in front of leadership, because it is the only one that measures the brand principle the whole strategy rests on: don't sell a policy without the right coverage amount.

Cover purchased ÷ cover recommended. Tracked as a distribution, not an average.

Conversion rate alone cannot distinguish between the two outcomes this release was designed to separate: more people buying adequate cover, and more people buying cheap cover faster. Both raise conversion. Only one is the strategy.

Read the distribution, not the mean

A mean ratio of 0.7 could be everyone buying roughly 70% of what they need, or half buying in full and half buying a token policy. Those demand opposite responses. Look at the shape — and specifically at the share of purchases below 0.5, which is the population the principle exists to protect.

05 — Metrics that will mislead you

What not to optimise.

Each of these is a plausible dashboard metric that, if improved, would push the product back toward what this release replaced.

Tempting metricWhy it misleads here
Recommendation CTRMaximised by making the pitch louder and higher. That is the banner strategy with a new name.
Time in appThis screen's job is to answer a question quickly. Rising dwell time is as likely to mean confusion as engagement.
Sessions per weekWidgets exist to reduce app opens. Falling sessions with stable actions is the widgets working.
Recommendation impressionsOptimised by showing more, to more people, more often — the definition of a promotional slot.
Number of features discoveredDiscovery was never the goal. Relevance was. A user who needs one capability and finds it is a success.
Policies added, in isolationSays nothing about whether the portfolio is now accurate. Pair it with document attachment and sync health.

The failure mode to name out loud

Six months from now, someone under pressure will propose raising the recommendation up the screen because CTR is low. Write down now that low CTR on a deliberately-subordinate element is the expected result, not a defect. Pre-committing to that reading is the only thing that will survive the meeting.

06 — Instrumentation

The one thing that must be logged.

Almost everything above is impossible without a single event property: the condition set that produced the surface. Log which conditions evaluated true, and which recommendation was chosen and at what rank.

Without the condition set you can never measure precision — only popularity.

Minimum event set

portfolio_viewed — state id, condition set, member count, policy count
recommendation_surfaced — type, triggering condition, rank, position
recommendation_reached — scrolled into view, dwell
recommendation_actioned — type, condition, action taken
policy_added — category, source (onboarding hint, manual, sync)
member_added — from family overview or elsewhere
kyp_opened — rating band, from card or from section
inactive_resolved — days since surfaced
share_completed — policy type, reason shown or not
advisor_booked — surface, whether post-report
purchase_completed — category, cover purchased, cover recommended, tier

Two derived tables make the rest trivial: a daily snapshot of each user's condition set, so transitions can be computed retrospectively; and a recommendation log preserving what was offered, not only what was taken. Both are cheap now and impossible to reconstruct later.

07 — What numbers cannot tell you

Three things to go and ask.

  • Precision audit
    Was the recommendation actually right? Take 50 users, look at their real portfolio, and judge by hand whether the surfaced recommendation was the best available next step. This is the only way to separate "the user ignored us" from "we were wrong". Repeat quarterly.
  • Comprehension
    Do they understand the number? Five users, one question: "what does this recommendation mean and where do you think it came from?" If they cannot trace it to CoverRisk, the evidence is not doing its job however good the conversion looks.
  • The non-actors
    Why didn't the people who saw it act? The most valuable and least contacted segment. Distinguish "not now", "not relevant", "didn't understand", and "didn't notice" — four causes with four different fixes and identical analytics signatures.
08 — Cadence

How this becomes the Adapt step for the team.

The product adapts to the user. The same loop should run one level up, on the strategy itself.

WhenLook atDecision it supports
WeeklyInstrumentation health, stale condition count, error and sync ratesIs the data trustworthy yet? Nothing else matters until yes
MonthlyTransition rate and prompt resolution, per stateWhich conditions are working; which are quietly ignored
QuarterlyCover adequacy distribution, precision audit, the hypothesis registerConfirm, revise or retire each claim in §03
Twice yearlyWhether the tiering still matches the portfolio shapes users actually haveUpdate the recommendation order itself

Sequence the first ninety days

  1. Days 1–30: instrumentation only. Resist reporting. A number from broken logging is worse than no number, because it gets quoted for a year.
  2. Days 30–60: establish per-state baselines. No targets yet — you cannot set a threshold for a distribution you have not seen.
  3. Days 60–90: fill in the disproof thresholds in §03, then run the first experiment. The two-actions ceiling is the cheapest and cleanest place to start.

If a claim in §03 cannot be tested a year from now, it was a preference, not a strategy.
