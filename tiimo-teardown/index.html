Competitive teardown · Product design

Tiimo makes time visible. That is the product, and also the liability.

Apple's iPhone App of the Year 2025 is a visual planner for neurodivergent users. Its design works because people hand it their executive function — which is precisely why its current commercial direction is worth worrying about.

Read this before the analysis

I have not used this app. No install, no real days planned, no reminder allowed to fire, no missed activity observed, no paywall encountered in context. Roughly a third of what the brief asks for — onboarding, information architecture, interaction feel, accessibility testing, tone of voice under failure — cannot be answered honestly from outside.

So those sections are not filled with plausible-sounding invention. They are marked hands-on required with the specific thing to look for when someone does open the app. Everything else is grounded in the company's own site, the EU funding record, press, and user reviews, and tagged accordingly.

What follows is therefore a strategic and structural read, plus a field guide — not a usability audit.

01 — Thesis

The value is not planning. It is outsourced executive function.

Tiimo's users are not organising tasks; they are externalising a cognitive process they cannot reliably run internally. Making time visible is the mechanism. That produces unusual dependence, and unusual loyalty.

It also inverts the normal tolerance for defects. When a to-do app is buggy, you lose a list. When this app is buggy, the user loses the scaffold they had stopped compensating for. Reliability is not a quality attribute here. It is the feature. Which is why streaks, an AI headline, and reported billing friction matter far more than they would elsewhere.

02 — Origin

It started as a caregiver tool for children. That explains a lot.

The EU research record is unambiguous, and it is the single most clarifying document I found. observed

"Tiimo mood: integrating end-user's self-assessment module in ADHD assistive technologies for the continuous enhancement of non-medical therapies"

Tiimo AS · H2020 SME Instrument Phase 1 · €50,000 EU contribution, €71,429 total · Dec 2018 – May 2019

CORDIS project 836101

That project describes gamified smartwatch technology for children with ADHD, with task reminders like "go to school" or "do your homework", and a web platform for caregivers managing activities. Its market sizing counts paediatric diagnoses and models savings in "educational assistance and family productivity losses". observed

Today's positioning is a different product for a different buyer: "Whether you're neurodivergent, self-diagnosed, or just someone who wants planning to feel easier, you're in the right place." observed That is an adult, self-directed, self-diagnosing consumer — not a clinician, teacher or parent.

Two things follow, and both are useful. inferred

  • Gamification is not a recent betrayal of principles — it is in the DNA. The original pitch was explicitly "gamified". Anyone reading today's streaks-and-trophies feature as a late-stage growth compromise has the history backwards.
  • Mood tracking is the surviving limb of a machine-learning research project, not a wellness trend. "Tiimood" was a self-assessment module feeding an ML model to find patterns supporting therapy. The current site still ships mood tracking and daily reflection. The data ambition may still be there underneath.

A company that moved from caregiver-mediated child assistive tech to adult self-serve consumer subscription has changed who it is accountable to.

03 — User and job

The stated job and the real job are not the same.

The stated job is "plan my day". The real job, judging by how the product is built and how users describe it, is closer to "make the day feel survivable before it starts" — and then, during it, "tell me what is happening now so I don't have to decide again." inferred

The distinction is not academic. A planning tool optimises for capture and organisation. A tool for the real job optimises for reducing the number of decisions between waking and doing. Those two briefs produce different products, and they explain why Tiimo's home surface is a timeline rather than an inbox.

Moment of need

The trigger is not "I have a new task". It is more likely one of: the day ahead is unstructured and that is producing anxiety; a transition has arrived and initiation has stalled; or time has passed unnoticed and the user needs re-anchoring. inferred Widgets and Live Activities observed exist to serve the second and third of those without requiring the app to be opened — which is itself an act of initiation the user may not manage.

Hands-on required

Secondary users are a real gap. The origin story says caregivers were once first-class. Does any of that survive — shared plans, a supporter view, export for a coach or clinician? If it was removed, that is a deliberate narrowing worth understanding. If it was never rebuilt for adults, it is an obvious wedge for a competitor.

The marketing headline is now "A visual AI planner built for real life." The distinguishing word — who it is for — has left the sentence.

07 — The streaks problem

A shame mechanic in an anti-shame product.

This is the sharpest finding in the teardown, and I want to be careful with it because it cuts against the brief's own assumption. The brief I was given listed "deliberately avoids shame mechanics — streaks, guilt-based nudges" as a hypothesis. That hypothesis is wrong as of now. The site promotes Streaks explicitly: "Build consistency and celebrate progress with trophies and rewards." observed

Why this matters more here than in a general-audience app: a streak is a stored quantity of past success that can be destroyed by a single lapse. For a user whose defining difficulty includes inconsistency, and whose emotional profile may include acute sensitivity to failure, a streak is a liability accumulator. It converts a bad Tuesday into a visible loss. Every ADHD-adjacent design discussion I am aware of treats this as a known hazard. inferred

Where I'd push back in the room

Two defences are available and I don't fully believe either. The first is that this is opt-in — plausible, unverified, and hands-on required to confirm. The second is that "celebrate progress" framing keeps it positive; but a mechanic's framing does not change what happens when it breaks. The question is not how the trophy is worded. It is what the app says on day two of a broken streak.

My read: this is retention engineering that survived a values review it should have failed. I would want to see the experiment data before accepting it. inferred

The origin research reframes it, though, and this is the honest complication: the 2018 EU project described the product as "gamified" from the outset. observed So streaks may be a return to type rather than a drift from principle — which makes the "anti-shame product" reputation partly a story told about Tiimo by its community rather than a constraint Tiimo ever adopted. inferred

08 — Monetisation

The boundary is drawn at frustration, not at value.

Pricing is reported at $7.99/month, $79.99/year, and a family plan at $119.99/year for up to five users. reported The family tier is unusual in this category and looks like a quiet echo of the caregiver origins.

The free/paid boundary is where I have a genuine evidence conflict I could not resolve. The company's own site lists the free tier as including "planning tools, to-do lists, focus timer". observed A third-party pricing summary states free excludes the web planner, AI assistant and focus timer. reported Those cannot both be true. If the focus timer is paywalled, the single clearest expression of the product's thesis — making time visible — is behind the paywall, and that is a materially different product for free users. hands-on required

Reported experience of the commercial layer is poor, and consistently so across sources: reported

  • An "overly aggressive premium upsell" described as detracting from the experience
  • Difficulty cancelling — one account of a ten-day support delay followed by being charged anyway
  • Slow or absent support responses to reported bugs

Aggressive upsell interrupts the one screen whose entire purpose is to reduce the number of decisions the user has to make.

That is not a generic dark-pattern complaint. It is a direct contradiction of the product's mechanism, delivered on the surface where the mechanism runs. And subscription friction lands hardest on a population for whom administrative follow-through is the diagnosed difficulty — a cancellation flow requiring persistence is, for this audience specifically, a revenue tactic that exploits the condition the product claims to serve. inferred I would put that sentence on a slide.

09 — Position

Two axes that actually discriminate.

Who should not use it

Anyone whose work is genuinely list-shaped rather than time-shaped — large backlogs, dependencies, delegation — is better served by Todoist or Things. Anyone who wants to be told what to do next should use Motion. And anyone who needs the tool to be boringly dependable above all else has, on the reported evidence, a legitimate reason to hesitate. inferred

10 — The bet

What this company believes that its competitors don't.

Stated plainly: that designing for the hardest case produces a better mainstream product than designing for the average case, and that the hard case will pay a premium for it. inferred

Their own framing supports it — "Tiimo was built by and for neurodivergent people from the start, not as a trend, but as a mission" observed — and Apple's iPhone App of the Year 2025 award is external validation of the thesis, not just of the craft. observed Claimed scale is over 3 million downloads and over 1 million users. reported — their own marketing, uncorroborated.

The bet is being partly walked back. Every recent signal — AI in the headline, streaks, App Store titles converging on "AI Planner" and "To-Do List" — points toward broadening from the hard case to the average one. inferred That is a familiar and dangerous transition: the specificity is the moat, and mainstream productivity is the most crowded category on the store.

The uncomfortable question

Is a "visual AI planner built for real life" defensible against Apple, Google, Notion or Motion adding a colour-coded timeline? Probably not. Is "the planner that neurodivergent people trust, and that their community actively evangelises" defensible? Considerably more so. The moat is the audience relationship, and the current positioning spends it. inferred

11 — Vulnerable audience

Where it holds a line, and where it slips.

Holds. Widgets and Live Activities are a genuine act of restraint: they deliver value while explicitly removing the need to open the app, which is the opposite of what an engagement-maximising product does with the same surface. observed Building for self-diagnosed users without requiring a diagnosis is also the right call, and quietly generous — it declines to gatekeep access behind a medical system many people cannot reach.

Slips. Three, in ascending order of seriousness. inferred

  1. Streaks and trophies introduce a failure state into a product used by people with heightened sensitivity to failure.
  2. Aggressive upselling on the primary surface spends the user's limited attention on the company's goal rather than theirs.
  3. Cancellation friction monetises a difficulty with administrative follow-through — the precise deficit the product exists to compensate for.

There is also an unexamined data question. The product collects mood and daily reflection alongside a strong implicit signal about neurotype, and its research lineage was explicitly about feeding a machine-learning model. observed What is retained, what trains models, and what a self-disclosure of neurodivergence is worth commercially are all unanswered here. assumed I would not present on this without reading the privacy policy properly.

Designing for a difficulty and designing around a diagnosis are different. Tiimo does the first well. The commercial layer occasionally does neither.

Where it breaks down.

Recurring, independently reported complaints. reported I have not verified any of them first-hand, and review corpora over-represent frustration by nature — but the consistency across sources is itself signal.

Copyable in a quarter

The colour-coded timeline, the visual timer, the widget strategy, template routines. None of it is technically hard. inferred

Hard to copy

Credibility with a community that has been marketed at badly for years, the accumulated design judgement about what not to include, and the Apple award as a trust signal. All three are reputational, and reputation is what streaks and upsell pressure spend.

14 — Transferable

What to steal, ranked by confidence.

  • high
    Design for the hardest user, then check it still works for the average one. Tiimo's constraints — low reading load, redundant encoding, tolerance for interruption — produce a product that is simply clearer for everyone. This is the generalisable lesson, and it is not a diversity argument; it is a design-quality argument.
  • high
    Deliver value on surfaces that don't require opening the app. Treat "user must launch the app" as a cost to be engineered away, not a metric to maximise. Widgets, watch complications and live activities as primary rather than satellite.
  • high
    Encode state redundantly. Colour plus icon plus text, so meaning survives colour-blindness, small screens, glances and cognitive load. Cheap to implement, disproportionate return.
  • medium
    Make the abstract quantity spatial. Time is the case here, but the pattern generalises to anything users reason about badly in the abstract — budget, risk, capacity, coverage. Render it as space and the judgement gets easier.
  • medium
    Templates beat blank canvases for anyone with low starting capacity. The first-run blank state is where this category loses people. A pre-filled example day is a stronger onboarding device than a tour.
  • low
    A specific audience is a real moat — while you keep it. Marked low confidence because Tiimo is currently testing the opposite proposition. Worth watching rather than copying: the next two years of their positioning will be a natural experiment in whether earned community credibility survives a mainstream pivot.

Do not copy

Streaks and trophies — and specifically, do not copy them because a respected accessibility-focused product does it. That is the worst possible reason. Award-winning products contain compromises too, and this looks like one.

Also do not copy the visual style as a shortcut to trustworthiness. Rounded, pastel and friendly is a surface. What earns the trust is the restraint underneath — the features they declined to ship, which you cannot see in a screenshot.

15 — Field guide

What to do in the app, in priority order.

These are the questions this teardown could not answer. Ranked by how much each would change the analysis. hands-on required

  1. Break a streak deliberately, then open the app the next day. Screenshot exactly what it says. This single screen tells you the product's real position on shame, and it decides §07.
  2. Check whether streaks can be turned off, and whether that is default-on or default-off. Default-on would be the tell.
  3. Resolve the focus-timer paywall conflict. Site says free includes it; a third party says it doesn't. If it is paid, the free tier does not contain the product's thesis.
  4. Miss an activity mid-day and note whether the plan silently reflows or accusingly persists. Reflow is the humane choice and the harder engineering.
  5. Time the first run to first planned day. Count screens, count questions asked before any value is returned, note whether an example day is offered.
  6. Test the AI on a genuinely vague input — "sort out the flat" — and check whether the time estimates are defensible. The complaint corpus says they often aren't.
  7. Run the accessibility pass properly: contrast ratios, largest dynamic type, reduce-motion, VoiceOver labels on the timeline, and whether any state is colour-only.
  8. Try to cancel. Document the flow. Given the reported experience, this is the most ethically loaded screen in the product.
  9. Leave it alone for a week, then return. The re-entry state after lapse is where this category earns or loses long-term users.
  10. Read the privacy policy on mood data and model training.
16 — Open questions

What I don't know.

  • Is the anti-shame reputation something Tiimo built, or something its community projected onto it? The gamified origin suggests the latter, which would substantially change how to read the current direction.
  • Are streaks opt-in, and what does a broken one look like? The hinge of §07.
  • What does the free tier actually contain? Sources conflict on the focus timer.
  • Did caregiver and shared-plan features survive the pivot? If not, is that a deliberate narrowing or an unguarded flank?
  • Is the AI feature retained or churned against? It is the headline and the most-complained-about feature simultaneously. Those cannot both persist.
  • What is the actual retention curve past 60 days? Planner apps are notorious for high install and low persistence, and no public number exists. Without it, every claim about the loop working is inference.
  • How much of the growth is community evangelism versus the Apple award? Determines how fragile the acquisition engine is.

How I'd resolve the most important one

Retention. No amount of teardown substitutes for it. Absent access to their data, the closest proxies are review recency patterns from long-term users, changelog priorities over 18 months as a proxy for where they think the leak is, and a small diary study with five ADHD users over six weeks. The last would cost little and answer more than everything above.
